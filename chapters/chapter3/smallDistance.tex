After testing a known structure, an attempt was made to change the geometry to test its impact on stability and to try to develop faster stabilizing and more stable devices. In this case, the channel of the standard EG-FET was repositioned closer to the gate, reducing the distance from \SI{1.5}{\mm} to \SI{100}{\um}, resulting in what is herein referred to as the \vv{small distance} EG-FET, or sdEG-FET. The hypothesis behind this change is that reducing the channel-gate distance could allow the gate to have greater control over the channel, potentially leading to faster stabilization compared to the standard geometry tested in Section \ref{sec:big_channel}. This section aims to evaluate the validity of this hypothesis by analyzing the transfer curves, normalized current, and other relevant parameters collected for the standard device, with and without the membrane.

Below I explain (1) the physical effects that change when you reduce the gap, (2) why your measurements can look almost identical, (3) plausible causes for the residual hysteresis in the short-gap device, and (4) a set of practical checks/measurements to sort out the origin of the effects.

\section*{1. What changing the distance does (physical intuition)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geometric (bulk) capacitance increases.}  
    If the gate and channel form two facing electrodes separated by an electrolyte-filled gap, the geometric capacitance scales roughly as $C_{\text{geom}}\approx \varepsilon A/d$ (area $A$, separation $d$). Reducing $d$ from 1.5 mm to 100 µm increases this geometric capacitance by $\sim$15$\times$, all other things equal.

    \item \textbf{The EDL still dominates the local interface, but the series picture matters.}  
    In electrolyte-gated devices the \emph{electrical double layer} (EDL) at the gate/electrolyte and channel/electrolyte interfaces typically has nm-scale thickness and therefore very large specific capacitance. The total effective gate capacitance is a series combination of the two EDL capacitances and the geometric (bulk) capacitance of the electrolyte gap. If the EDL capacitances are much larger than the geometric capacitance, the geometric term becomes the bottleneck --- so decreasing $d$ reduces that bottleneck and increases effective gate coupling.

    \item \textbf{Solution (ionic) resistance and RC time constants drop.}  
    Smaller gap $\rightarrow$ shorter ion travel paths $\rightarrow$ lower solution resistance $R_s$. That reduces the RC time constant $ \tau \sim R_s C$ that controls how fast the EDL and channel equilibrate. Faster equilibration tends to reduce dynamic effects (hysteresis caused by slow ion redistribution).

    \item \textbf{Smaller voltage drop in the bulk electrolyte.}  
    With a large gap, a portion of the applied gate potential drops across the electrolyte bulk (ohmic/IR drop). Reducing the distance reduces that drop, so more of the applied potential actually appears across the EDL and therefore more effectively gates the channel.

    \item \textbf{Risks / potential negatives.}  
    Bringing gate very close increases risk of:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item ionic shorting or electrolyte bridging if the membrane/spacer is imperfect,
        \item increased parasitic coupling or stray capacitance to other structures,
        \item stronger local fields that might accelerate electrochemical side reactions if you push voltage limits.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{2. Why you might \emph{not} see big differences in transfer curves or stabilization time}
Although the geometric arguments above make it plausible you should see a difference, there are good reasons the measured transfer curves are similar:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{EDL-dominated regime:} if the EDL capacitances are already comparable to or smaller than the geometric capacitance at 1.5 mm, reducing $d$ further may only modestly change the total series capacitance. 
    \item \textbf{Both distances $\gg$ Debye length \& nm scale:} the active gating action happens at nm scales (EDL), so millimetre-to-sub-millimetre changes are sometimes second-order for the steady-state transfer characteristic. 
    \item \textbf{Measurement conditions dominate:} if hysteresis/stabilization is dominated by slow traps in the CNTs, contact effects, or by membrane-local ionic/permeation kinetics rather than bulk ionic resistance, then reducing the bulk gap may not strongly change the measured steady-state transfer curve.
    \item \textbf{Practical fabrication / variability:} device-to-device variation, small differences in membrane placement, or tiny defects can mask the expected systematic trends.
\end{itemize}

So: a smaller gap changes the electrodynamics and speed of response, but not necessarily the DC transfer curve amplitude if the EDL or other bottlenecks already dominate.

\section*{3. Why hysteresis in the short-gap device might remain}
You mentioned that the short-distance device retains some hysteresis at the end. Possible causes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fabrication / local defects} (e.g., incomplete sealing, pinholes, residues) could cause inhomogeneity.
    \item \textbf{Local trapped ions under the membrane} --- ions may be confined and relax very slowly.
    \item \textbf{Enhanced local electrochemical activity} due to stronger fields at the smaller gap.
    \item \textbf{Measurement artifacts} (contact, grounding, instrument settling).
\end{itemize}
Given your statement that transfer curves and stabilization times are otherwise similar, fabrication/measurement inconsistency is a plausible simple explanation, but trapped ions or enhanced local reactions are physically plausible too.

\section*{4. Practical checks to distinguish causes}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Repeatability across multiple short-gap devices.
    \item Sweep-rate dependence: test hysteresis vs sweep speed.
    \item Rest/relaxation test after unbiased recovery.
    \item Gate leakage / faradaic current monitoring.
    \item Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) to separate $R_s$, C$_{\text{EDL}}$, and charge-transfer resistance.
    \item $I_{\text{on}}/I_{\text{off}}$ vs time traces.
    \item Vary electrolyte ionic strength.
    \item Optical/microscopy inspection of membranes.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{5. Suggested wording for manuscript/report}
\begin{quote}

In our measurements the steady-state transfer characteristics and stabilization time were very similar between the two separations, indicating that the interface EDL capacitance and other bottlenecks (for example, trap dynamics or membrane-limited ion permeation) dominate the DC gating response. \end{quote}

\section*{6. Final practical note}
Because the EDL is so thin, bulk geometric changes only matter when they push the series geometric capacitance or ionic resistance into the same order as the interfacial capacitances. The change from 1.5 mm to 100 µm may be in a regime where the geometric term is still subdominant for DC gating, but should manifest most clearly in dynamic tests (EIS, sweep-rate) or under more aggressive gating conditions. These experiments will reveal whether the shorter gap gives any real advantage for sensing performance in this architecture.

\section*{Possible causes (with short explanations and quick checks)}

\subsection*{1. Higher $I_{\text{off}}$ (worse OFF current) due to parasitic leakage or trapped ions}
\textbf{Mechanism:} bringing the gate very close can create small regions where the membrane is thinner, has pinholes, or traps ions under it. These local ionic pathways or trapped charges can increase leakage in depletion, raising $I_{\text{off}}$ and reducing the ratio.  
\textbf{Check:} plot $I_{\text{off}}$ vs time for both devices. If the small-gap device has a consistently larger $I_{\text{off}}$, this is likely.

\subsection*{2. Stronger fringing / non-uniform fields $\rightarrow$ incomplete depletion}
\textbf{Mechanism:} at very short distances the electric field geometry and fringing fields change; the gate may couple more strongly to some parts of the channel than others, causing incomplete or non-uniform depletion and a higher residual current in OFF.  
\textbf{Check:} map $I_{\text{on}}$ and $I_{\text{off}}$ spatially (if possible) or measure multiple devices to see reproducibility; look also at subthreshold slope---worse slope may signal non-uniform gating.

\subsection*{3. Increased parasitic capacitance or screening that reduces effective modulation}
\textbf{Mechanism:} the reduced gap increases geometric capacitance and can also increase parasitic capacitances to other structures. In series with the EDL this may change the effective modulation in a way that raises $I_{\text{off}}$ or lowers $I_{\text{on}}$ slightly.  
\textbf{Check:} EIS or capacitance measurements to compare total gate capacitance and frequency response for the two distances.

\subsection*{4. Altered EDL formation under the membrane (different ionic configuration)}
\textbf{Mechanism:} the membrane+short gap geometry can change how the double layer forms (different ion partitioning, trapped water pockets, slower ion transport under the membrane). This can raise $I_{\text{off}}$ or lower $I_{\text{on}}$ slightly.  
\textbf{Check:} vary ionic strength (higher salt tends to reduce EDL thickness effects); if the small-gap device responds differently, the EDL is implicated. Rest/relaxation tests also help.

\subsection*{5. Slightly increased contact / series resistance affecting $I_{\text{on}}$}
\textbf{Mechanism:} if fabrication for the small-gap device introduced slight differences at source/drain contacts (e.g., stress during membrane placement), $I_{\text{on}}$ could be reduced compared to the standard device. Because the on/off ratio depends on both currents, a small drop in $I_{\text{on}}$ lowers the ratio.  
\textbf{Check:} extract contact resistance (TLM or linear-regime fit) before/after the tests; compare $I_{\text{on}}$ absolute values.

\subsection*{6. Measurement / device-to-device variability}
\textbf{Mechanism:} the difference (24 $\rightarrow$ 20) is small and could be simply within fabrication or measurement spread. Small differences in CNT coverage, membrane positioning, or micro-defects can easily change the ratio by this magnitude.  
\textbf{Check:} measure several devices of each type and report mean $\pm$ std. If the difference persists statistically, it is real; if not, it’s variability.

\subsection*{7. Slight electrochemical side effects (local redox, parasitic currents)}
\textbf{Mechanism:} closer gate may increase local field causing tiny faradaic currents at defects; these add to $I_{\text{off}}$ or distort $I_{\text{on}}$.  
\textbf{Check:} monitor gate current during sweeps; significant gate leakage suggests electrochemistry.

\section*{Recommended priority of checks (fast $\rightarrow$ more involved)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Plot $I_{\text{on}}$ and $I_{\text{off}}$ vs time separately for both device types.  
    \item Check gate leakage during sweeps.  
    \item Repeat the measurement on multiple nominally identical devices to estimate spread.  
    \item Do a sweep-rate test and a brief rest/recovery test.  
    \item Run EIS to get $R_s$ and $C$ components if needed.  
    \item If still unclear, check contacts (TLM) and inspect membrane under optical/SEM.  
\end{enumerate}

Considering all the factors discussed above, the conclusion is that while the performance of the sdEG-FET is not necessarily poor, the standard EG-CNTFET with the membrane is better. Indeed, the standard EG-CNTFET is faster in reaching equilibrium and is easier to work with. The smaller channel-to-gate distance in the sdEG-FET makes it challenging to dropcast the membrane without compromising the gate. If the gate is partially covered, the charge accumulation is altered, leading to reduced control over the channel and a subsequent decrease in the output current. Therefore the sdEG-FET does not present an ideal solution for our sensing applications.
