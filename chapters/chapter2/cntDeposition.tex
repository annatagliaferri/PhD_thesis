Spray-coating is a technique that exploits atomization to deposit thin, uniform
layers of material. Following the schematics in Figure \ref{fig:sprayCoater} it
is possible to better understand how this process works: a liquid ink (the
CNT-containing solution) is stored in the reservoir, then it is transferred to
the nozzle where it collides with compressed air which atomizes it. The droplets
are then extruded from the nozzle onto the substrate, which is positioned on a
hot plate, causing the solvent to evaporate during the process; this leaves only
the thin semiconductor film. Adjusting the main parameters, such as flow rate,
gas pressure, hot plate temperature, and nozzle-to-substrate distance, it is
possible to optimize the film properties as needed
\citep{shkodraElectrolytegated2021,shkodraOptimization2023}.

First, a stock CNT dispersion was created by combining \SI{0.05}{\%} wt SWCNTs
in \ce{diH2O} with \SI{0.5}{\%} wt CMC as a surfactant. This was sonicated using
a horn sonicator in alternating cycles of \SI{5}{\min} at \SI{50}{\%} and
\SI{30}{\%} power, for a total of \SI{25}{\min}, to ensure complete dispersion.
To conclude stock preparation, the tube was centrifuged at \SI{13000}{rpm} for
\SI{100}{\min}; the stock was then diluted 1:30 with \SI{1.3}{mM} CMC to reach
the final concentration needed for spray-coating.

Prior to deposition, the electrodes needed to be cleaned and treated to enhance
their wettability: to this aim they were placed in oxygen plasma at
\SI{100}{\watt} for \SI{1}{\min}. A metallic shadow mask was used to only expose
the IDE area. The devices were then ready for the spray coating process, whose
parameters were previously optimized by \citet{shkodraOptimization2023}: the
nozzle-to-substrate distance was set at \SI{5}{\cm}, the atomizing pressure at
\SI{0.5}{\bar}, the material feed pressure at \SI{0.2}{\bar}, and the arm speed
at \SI{150}{\mm\per\s}. The spraying process occurred while the substrate was
laying flat on a hotplate heated to \SI{70}{\celsius}, to favour water
evaporation. The deposition process was also optimized, being performed in 10
steps, with each step applying 6 layers, thus resulting in a total of 60 layers.
A \SI{1}{\min} pause was left between each step to allow the solvent to
evaporate and promote a more uniform SWCNT deposition.

The quality of the spray was initially assessed using an optical microscope
immediately after deposition; at this stage, only the solvent was visible,
appearing as circular \vv{bubbles} on the surface, making it possible to
estimate the deposited CNT amount (see Figure \ref{fig:CNTopticalMicroscope}).

Afterwards, the samples underwent treatment in \SI{2.90}{M} nitric acid for
\SI{1}{\hour} at room temperature to remove the surfactant and expose the
SWCNTs. The substrates were then rinsed in a water bath for \SI{30}{\min} and
dried inverted on a hotplate at \SI{100}{\celsius} for \SI{30}{\min}. At this
point, the SWCNTs could be further characterized through atomic force microscopy
(AFM, see Figure \ref{fig:AFM}) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, see
Figure \ref{fig:SEM}). More details about morphological characterization are
given in Section \ref{sec:morphoCaracterization}. As a last check on CNT
quality, the resistance between source and drain was measured and if deemed
adequate, the devices were stored in a nitrogen-filled glovebox to protect them
from degradation in air, before any subsequent analyses.
